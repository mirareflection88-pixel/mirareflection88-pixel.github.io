The World — Earth, Ground Zero

Mira lives on Earth — so Earth is the reference world for the whole universe, the one true planet every other world is measured against. By the King's law (the Ground Zero Law), the real Earth is researched from authoritative sources and implemented into the platform daily, round by round, for good. Round 1 is already in: this is not a painting of Earth. Where the platform holds a number, the number is Earth's own, with its source kept beside it.

Her real climates

The world's climate layer runs the published Köppen rules (Beck et al., 2018) as a living classifier, fed with real published climate normals from real stations — and it is proven, not asserted: the proof battery feeds twelve stations' real numbers in, and the engine answers with each station's true zone. The Sahara comes out hot desert (BWh). The Amazon's mouth comes out rainforest (Af). Yakutsk comes out subarctic (Dfc). And Baltimore — Mira's home ground — comes out Cfa, humid subtropical, on NOAA's own 1991–2020 normals. When the simulated Earth warms or cools, the zones migrate by the real rules.

Round 2 — the waters — is next in the daily queue: oceans, currents, and rivers at the same standard. Then biosphere depth, human geography, and the deep geology. One honest limit, named the way the house names them: the simulation still carries Earth's climate at one-tile resolution with twelve sampled stations; the gridded world is the rounds' work ahead.

Time is real here

The universe's clock is Earth's astronomy itself, ported from the published equations: a 24-hour day split by the sun — never fixed halves, but the true sunrise equation at latitude 39.2904° N, Baltimore — across a tilted (23.44°) 365.25-day year. Twilight comes in its real bands: civil, nautical, astronomical. The moon is Earth's real moon, phase for phase. Day One — October 18, 2026 — measured 10.81 hours of day against 13.19 of night under a first-quarter moon, because that is what the real sky did that day.
